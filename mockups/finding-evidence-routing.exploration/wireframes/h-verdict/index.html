Round H — the verdict, ahead of the roster

Three shapes for one finding's occurrences, drawn on the production evidence table. Each answers the same question — did this habit hold? — and each pays for the answer differently. The frames are stacked so they can be read against each other; the state switch moves all three at once, so resting is compared with resting and drilled with drilled.

Form
State
Theme

The drilled state opens the same occurrence in all three forms: the largest swing in the set, which is a near-rule occurrence. It is the honest probe — the worst-looking low here is one the habit did not claim, and each form makes reaching it cost something different.

H1 — verdict leads. The answer is a sentence at the top of the column; the roster is what backs it up.

H2 — one ranked list. No sections. Seventeen occurrences in one order, each carrying its verdict as an inline mark.

H3 — proportional band, one verdict at a time. The split is a figure you read in one glance; the roster shows exactly one verdict's occurrences.

What each form costs

H1 — verdict leads
Buys: the reader learns the answer before meeting a row, in their own words, and the answer stays on screen while they drill. Costs: four lines of the column's height, every visit, forever — including the twentieth visit, when the reader already knows. The sentence is also the one thing on the surface that has to be written per finding kind, so it is the piece most likely to go stale or read as a claim the model cannot support. Serves: the person who opened the finding to find out whether it is real.
H2 — one ranked list
Buys: the strongest comparison — seventeen occurrences on one spine, in one order, so the reader can see at a glance that the worst swing is not one the habit claimed. Nothing is hidden and nothing is a container. Costs: no summary at all — the three-way split has to be counted off the marks, and the near-rule hedge loses its natural home (a group header) and has to hang off the cap line. Ranking by swing also invites the reading that the order is a severity ranking, which it is not. Serves: the person comparing their own occurrences against each other.
H3 — proportional band
Buys: the whole split as one figure, in one line of height, and a roster that is never longer than one verdict — the shortest column of the three, and the only form that scales to a finding with sixty occurrences. Costs: the reader can only ever see one verdict's occurrences, so cross-verdict comparison — the thing that made the near-rule outlier visible in H2 — takes a deliberate switch, and the switch looks like a filter on a canvas that is deliberately never filtered. That mismatch is a real risk against the settled ruling, and the frame states it. Serves: the person who already trusts the finding and wants the shape of the evidence, not the evidence.
