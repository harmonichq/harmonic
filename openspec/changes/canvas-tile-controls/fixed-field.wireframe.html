Wireframe — no fidelity claim — not lockable

The fixed field — the two mechanisms ADR 215 leaves open

ADR 215 rules the direction: the field is fixed at one focal chart and three minis, a pin is membership of a mini position (cap 3, focal never pinnable), unpinned minis seat by findings rank, and the chart explorer demotes to a view-all slide-out anchored by the dock row. Nothing above reopens. Two mechanisms it does not settle are drawn below, one question each.

Measured on 8877 at a 1440 viewport, before any change: canvas pane 1010×790, tile field 1010×550, focal tile 994×381, each mini 326×145, inspector pane 430 wide, the dock 429×98 sitting on the inspector's floor. The field already renders one focal plus three minis at zero pins, so the fixed geometry is what a reader sees today — this slice removes the machinery that could take it somewhere else, it does not move the default. The specimens below are that layout at roughly 0.6×.

Both grounds are drawn for every specimen. Held ink in this idiom is a filled glyph at full opacity among ghosted outlines — no hue — so the only way to judge a membership mark is to see it on bone and on graphite at once.

Q1 — the focal position cannot be held. What does its rail say?

Every mini carries a pushpin: solid means this position is the reader's and re-seating cannot take it. The focal position is never pinnable — a finding click or a mini tab always owns it — so the pushpin on the biggest tile in the field has no job. Four answers, differing in kind. Press the pins. Each specimen opens with mini 1 already pinned.

A1

The focal rail is shorter

kind: the control is absent where it is meaningless

The pushpin exists on a mini and nowhere else. The focal tile's rail carries maximize and alignment only. A control is drawn where it acts, and a reader never presses a pin that cannot hold.

Cost: the same chart's rail changes shape as it moves. Pin a mini, then tab it into focus: the pin you just set disappears from the tile — the membership survives underneath, but the tile stops saying so, and the only evidence left is the mini that took its place.

Pin, then click a mini's name to tab it into focus.

A2

The pin is a demotion

kind: the control keeps its glyph and gains a move

Pressing the pin on the focal tile sends that chart down into a mini position and holds it there, and the top-ranked mini rises into focus. One verb the whole field understands: pin means "keep this", and keeping something means it stops being the thing you are currently reading.

Cost: one glyph both holds and moves, so a press meant to keep rearranges the field under the reader's eye. It is also the only control on the surface whose effect depends on which position it sits in.

Press the focal tile's pin and watch the field.

A3

Present and dead

kind: the control is drawn, disabled, and explains itself

The rail is identical in all four positions; the focal tile's pin is simply disabled, titled "The focal position always follows your last click." The rail never changes shape, so nothing about a tile moves when the tile does.

Cost: a permanently dead control on the largest tile in the field — the one place a reader looks first. It teaches the rule by refusing, which is the weakest way to teach anything.

Hover the focal tile; try its pin.

A4

Membership belongs to the mini row

kind: the register is the furniture, not the tile

The three minis are drawn as one three-slot register — a hairline under the row, engraved HELD at its right end — and the pushpin lives only inside it. The focal tile sits above the register entirely, so there is no position there to hold and no control missing from it. The rule is drawn once, in the field, instead of four times in four rails.

Cost: new furniture on a field this slice is otherwise stripping — one more line and one more word on a surface that just lost the arrangement schematic. It also only reads as a register while all three minis are present.

Pin and unpin the minis; note the focal tile is outside the rule.

Q2 — the view-all roster is anchored by the dock row. How far does it come up?

The pull sits on the dock row (Trial · Watching · Profile) in all three. What differs is what the sheet covers — and with it whether "opening it must not repaint the seated charts" is true by construction or merely true if the code is careful. Each specimen prints, at its foot, what happens to the four seated charts when the sheet opens. Press the pull.

B1

Over the inspector only

kind: the roster is a list at the address it was pulled from

The sheet rises over the inspector pane and stops at the crumb. The canvas is never touched: not covered, not resized, not re-laid-out, so no seated chart can repaint no matter what the code does. At 430px the roster is a named list with a pushpin per row, not thumbnails.

Cost: no thumbnails, so "browse every registered chart" is browsing names. It also hides the findings queue — the ranked list the seating you are overriding comes from — for as long as you are browsing.

Pull. Pin two rows; watch the field not move.

B2

Over the tile field

kind: the roster claims the surface it seats into

The sheet rises from the dock row and covers the tile field, leaving the glucose strip and the whole inspector standing. Thumbnails are readable at that width, and you pin into the field while looking at where the chart will land. Seated tiles are covered, not unmounted.

Cost: the pull is on the inspector's floor and the sheet opens on the other pane — the gesture and its effect are 600px apart. And "does not repaint" is now a property of the code (tiles hidden, chart mounts kept) rather than of the geometry, so it needs a test to hold.

Pull. Pin two thumbnails; note the field under the sheet.

B3

Across both panes

kind: browsing charts is somewhere you go

The sheet is hinged on the dock row and comes up across the whole workstation below the glucose strip. The roster gets the full width, the largest thumbnails of the three, and reads as a place rather than a drawer.

Cost: the surface goes away to browse it — queue, dock and field all covered — which is very close to the second mode ADR 215 just retired, wearing different clothes.

Pull.
