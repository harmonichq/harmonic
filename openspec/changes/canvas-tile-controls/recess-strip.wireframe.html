The strip runs

The idea. Take the filmstrip literally: the dock is one continuous object running off both edges of the field, and the spotlight is the frame currently in the gate. So the well loses its ends — it bleeds through the left and right field edges and drops the ring that closed it — and its depth stops being a fill value and becomes a lit lip that runs unbroken across the whole width, dark line and falloff at the top, light line at the bottom. The 8px gutters collapse to a 1px frame line and the strip's margins move onto the cells, so the line runs the full height of the strip the way a frame line does on film. The selected frame is registered by the strip's own anatomy: its two flanking frame lines take the accent and close across its top edge, a gate open downward pointing at the chart on stage above it. In light the well also takes a real fill step down, because the reported zero-value step is a fact the running edge alone does not fix.

The cost. Frames butting on a hairline puts two plots a pixel apart, so a dense trace can read across the join before the neighbour's nameplate catches it — the price of continuity, paid in the one place a filmstrip always pays it. The full bleed also spends the field's left and right padding, so the strip is the one element on the canvas that touches the pane edges, and the gate's two verticals go one mark past the amendment's "one accent hairline along the cell's top edge, and nothing else."

At rest, docked, third frame in the gate. Order is the published rank and does not know what is on stage; the fourth frame is cut by the field edge and that slice is the only "there is more" this surface draws.
